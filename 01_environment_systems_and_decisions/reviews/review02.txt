Overall assessment
------------------
The primary headline values in Tables 3--6 mostly reproduce data/threeway_summary.csv, and the
manuscript compiles. However, the marked-up revision is not yet internally consistent enough to
submit. The most important problems are not typographical: the carbon-measurement boundary does
not match the units claimed in the paper; the claimed equivalence between fastest and lowest-carbon
architectures is contradicted by the latest-frame results; the real-case camera identities and
payload definitions are inconsistent with the released recorder documentation; and the current
reproducibility artifact does not contain the newly claimed real-case code/data/figures.

Findings requiring correction
=============================

1. HIGH -- Carbon tracker includes setup/warm-up work, not only the reported measured operation.
STATUS: COMPLETED (the manuscript now explicitly defines and labels the value as an amortised
cell cost, including setup, warm-up, reset, and accounting work; the underlying measurement is not
an isolated warm-database operation).
Manuscript lines 185--186 and Table 6, lines 293--313, say that each carbon value is the energy of
one measured run (2,000 inserts, one 2,000-image retrieval, or one latest-frame read), obtained by
dividing a tracker covering five runs by five. The implementation does not have that boundary:
code/run.py:173--187 places the tracker around engine.run_insert/run_retrieval/run_point_read,
and code/engine_base.py:139--150 shows that run_insert also performs warm-up inserts, resets the
database before every run, storage-size queries, and row-count queries. run_retrieval similarly
includes preparation, retrieval warm-ups, and cleanup (engine_base.py:156--175). These costs are
then divided by five and presented as the cost of one measured operation. This affects the SCI,
per-frame, cumulative, and annual values, not merely a label.

Required action: either move the tracker inside the timed measured-run loop and explicitly exclude
setup/warm-up work, or redefine the reported unit as an amortised cell cost including the stated
setup. Recompute/rename all downstream values accordingly and document whether warm-up and reset
energy is intended to be part of the operational footprint.

2. HIGH -- Reported throughput and carbon use different insertion boundaries.
STATUS: COMPLETED (the manuscript now discloses the pre-commit throughput boundary and says it is
not used to explain the carbon ordering).
The PostgreSQL and PostgreSQL+MinIO timers stop before conn.commit(): code/engine_postgres.py:88--105
and code/engine_postgres_minio.py:103--123. The CarbonTracker, in contrast, continues through the
commit and the surrounding reset/storage/accounting calls. Thus the manuscript compares a timed
throughput that excludes commit against carbon that includes commit and setup. This is especially
important for the hybrid, whose two-step write and transaction completion are part of the claimed
operational trade-off (lines 179, 655--659).

Required action: include commit in the reported insertion timing for all engines, or explicitly
state that throughput is an application-side pre-commit measure and do not use it to explain the
carbon ordering. Align the carbon and latency measurement regions before making performance/carbon
causality claims.

3. HIGH -- The statement that the fastest architecture is always the lowest-carbon architecture
is false for the paper's own tables.
STATUS: COMPLETED (the universal statement was replaced with operation-specific exceptions for
latest-frame reads).
Lines 526--529 claim that, for every operation and payload, the lowest-carbon architecture is also
the fastest, and use this to infer that finishing sooner almost always means emitting less. Table 4
(lines 260--267) and Table 8 (lines 348--355) provide counterexamples for latest-frame reads:

  * 480p: PostgreSQL is fastest (0.97 ms), but MongoDB has lower carbon (0.03 mg versus 0.05 mg).
  * 720p: PostgreSQL+MinIO is fastest (1.22 ms), but MongoDB has lower carbon (0.05 mg versus
    0.06 mg).
  * 360p: PostgreSQL+MinIO is fastest (1.09 ms), while MongoDB is lowest-carbon (0.03 mg versus
    0.05 mg for PostMin).

Energy is power integrated over time; lower elapsed time does not imply lower energy when power
differs. Required action: remove the universal statement and report operation-specific winners,
or provide a measured power/energy analysis that supports a narrower claim.

4. HIGH -- “Greenest” changes between sections because the workload weighting changes without
being made explicit early enough.
STATUS: PARTIALLY COMPLETED (the manuscript now distinguishes equal-operation totals from
ingestion-only deployment figures, but every headline “greenest/choice/crossover” statement still
needs its objective stated explicitly).
Table 9/lines 467--483 reports total carbon (insertion + bulk retrieval + latest-frame read), where
the 1080p real Tapo run gives MongoDB 31.59 mg versus PostgreSQL 34.99 mg. Lines 456--458 therefore
say MongoDB is greenest through 1080p. Table 14/lines 622--646 instead uses insertion-only carbon,
where the same 0.149 MB payload gives PostgreSQL 0.0110 mg/frame versus MongoDB 0.0142 mg/frame,
and lines 640--642 recommend PostgreSQL for continuous ingestion. Both results can be valid for
different operation mixes, but the manuscript currently presents both as the architecture selected
for “the” workload and uses different crossover interpretations (total versus insertion-only).

Required action: name the objective in every “greenest/choice/crossover” statement and formalise the
operation weights. Explain in the abstract, decision table, and real-case section that the result
changes from MongoDB under the equal one-of-each-operation profile to PostgreSQL under write-only
continuous retention.

5. HIGH -- The two-host comparison is described as software-identical even though the environments
are not software-identical.
STATUS: PARTIALLY COMPLETED (the environment differences are now acknowledged, but the remaining
hardware-independence wording for the real-frame ordering is still too strong).
Lines 389, 397, and 506 say software versions/protocol are identical and that differences are
attributable to the host. Table 11 (lines 490--506) lists different OS versions, Docker/Compose
versions, and Python versions: Ubuntu 24.04.4/Docker 29.4.0/Compose 5.1.1/Python 3.14 on the
controlled host versus Ubuntu 24.04.3/Docker 29.7.2/Compose 5.4.0/Python 3.14.7 on the validation
host. The raw CSVs also record Linux 6.8.0-117 and Python 3.14.5 for the i9 host versus Linux
6.8.0-90 and Python 3.14.7 for the i7 host.

Required action: say that the database images and benchmark protocol were held constant, while OS,
container runtime, Python patch level, SSD, CPU, and RAM differed. Do not attribute the entire
difference to hardware. The claim that real-frame ordering is hardware-independent is also too
strong: the real-frame runs are on the i7 host; the i9 host has the collage runs, not the same real
corpus.

6. HIGH -- The real-case camera identities are reversed relative to the recorder documentation and
the data directories.
STATUS: COMPLETED (the table now identifies the external 1080p and built-in 720p USB cameras
correctly).
Table 10 (lines 448--450) labels the 1920x1080, 2,988-frame, 157 KB stream as “USB camera 1” and
the 1280x720, 2,891-frame, 69 KB stream as “USB camera 2.” The released
code_realcase/README.md:117--139 says the opposite: Webcam 1 (/dev/video0) is the built-in
1280x720 camera, while Webcam 2 (/dev/video4) is the external 1920x1080 camera. The benchmark
directories support the documentation: data_frames_webcam1 has only through 720p, whereas
data_frames_webcam2 has 1080p results.

Required action: swap the USB-camera labels (and verify the frame counts/payload means), then
recheck lines 432 and 516. The “12% at 1080p” comparison appears to use the networked-camera
representative 0.149 MB versus the external-webcam representative 0.131 MB, but Table 10 reports
164 KB and 157 KB; those are not the same comparison.

7. HIGH -- “Mean payload” in Table 10 is not the payload recorded in the benchmark CSVs.
STATUS: COMPLETED (the table now distinguishes mean recorded size from the resized/re-encoded
benchmark payload and reports dispersion).
Table 10 calls Payload the mean encoded size and reports 164 KB for the networked camera. The
benchmark summary used in the manuscript records 0.148711 MB (about 149 KB) for Tapo 1080p and
0.131483 MB (about 131 KB) for webcam2. This follows from code/payloads.py:103--105: the
FrameSequenceSource reports the single frame closest to the mean, not the arithmetic mean. The
same representative value is then written into the result CSVs by code/results.py:86--123.

Required action: report the true mean (and preferably standard deviation/range) if Table 10 is to
say “mean,” or call the benchmark value a representative/medoid payload. Recalculate the crossover
and annual application using a clearly defined size statistic. This also explains why the text
alternates between 0.149 MB and 164 KB for the same camera.

9. MEDIUM-HIGH -- The repetition experiment does not support the wording “only MongoDB is affected”
or “slightly cheaper” for PostgreSQL/MinIO.
STATUS: PARTIALLY COMPLETED (the wording now acknowledges that all architectures change and
emphasises MongoDB's opposite-direction effect; exact uncertainty and a fully matched-size design
remain outstanding).
Table 13 (lines 572--589) calls only MongoDB affected, but the measured repeated-to-distinct ratios
for PostgreSQL and PostMin are 0.81/0.61 at about 0.09 MB and 0.75/0.53 at about 0.15 MB. Those are
19--47% changes, not slight differences. Moreover, the “matched” payloads are not identical: the
repeated values come from the i7 collage profiles while distinct values come from resized camera
frames, and the manuscript itself admits at lines 458 that size and distinctness are not fully
separable.

Required action: change the caption/text to say that MongoDB shows the largest and opposite-direction
change, while the other two also vary across the compared payloads; report exact byte sizes and
uncertainty. Do not attribute all PostgreSQL/MinIO differences to harmless noise without repeated
energy measurements or a tighter matched-size design.

10. MEDIUM-HIGH -- The i7 “crossover” is asserted from a near-tie below the reported variability.
STATUS: COMPLETED (the manuscript now calls the 1440p result a near-tie and does not treat it as
an established precise crossover).
At 1440p in Table 7 (line 411), PostMin is 0.1274 mg/frame and MongoDB is 0.1287 mg/frame: a 1.0%

Required action: call this a near-tie/inconclusive ordering, or provide independent repeated energy
runs and uncertainty intervals. Do not claim that the smaller-host crossover is established at

11. MEDIUM-HIGH -- The 6K “infeasible on any hardware/no tuning can remove it” claim is broader
than the experiment.
STATUS: PARTIALLY COMPLETED (the detailed BSON section is qualified to the tested configuration,
but the abstract, conclusion, and contribution language remain broader than that qualification).
The observed error is real and documented in data/skipped.csv: a 7.132 MB payload produced a
22.436 MB bucket object and hit MongoDB error 10334. However, the test fixes one time-series schema,
one metadata cardinality (all rows use device_id 1), default time-series bucketing settings, and a
particular batch/workload. The manuscript establishes infeasibility for this tested native
time-series configuration, not necessarily for every possible bucket-span/bucket-cardinality
configuration or schema around the payload. The BSON document ceiling itself is immutable, but
whether several frames are combined into one over-limit bucket is workload/configuration dependent.

Required action: qualify lines 386, 553, 594, 669, and 677 as “under the tested native time-series
schema/bucketing configuration,” report the relevant bucket settings, and separate the immutable
16 MiB BSON limit from the empirically observed bucket-formation failure. Also use MiB consistently:
the error says 16,793,600 bytes, i.e. 16 MiB, not 16 decimal MB.

12. MEDIUM -- Embodied-storage arithmetic mixes GB and GiB.
STATUS: COMPLETED (the manuscript now uses decimal GB consistently and recomputes the embodied
figures accordingly).
Line 361 says 2,000 5K frames occupy about 9.9 GB, while the raw payload is 5,333,253 bytes per
frame. That is about 9.93 GiB (or 10.67 decimal GB). The reported PostgreSQL/Mongo storage values

Required action: choose decimal GB throughout or label the values GiB and convert the embodied
intensity consistently. Recompute 4.8/1.6 kg if the source factor is defined in decimal GB.

SECOND PASS -- PAPER-FOCUSED AUDIT AND REVIEW01 CLOSURE
========================================================
This pass checks the manuscript as a paper: argument, literature positioning, scope of claims,
generalizability, practical meaning, limitations, declarations, and presentation. It deliberately
does not repeat the earlier code/data audit except where a paper statement depends on it.

Overall closure judgement
-------------------------
Review01 is substantially addressed, but not fully closed. The manuscript now contains visible
responses to all five editorial requests. The literature and ESD additions are real, the abstract
now has limitations, and the manuscript includes a second-host comparison plus a worked deployment
application. The remaining weaknesses are mainly claim calibration: the manuscript sometimes calls
joint evidence a test of hardware/software independence, calls a scenario projection a deployment
result, and describes a measurement procedure as a transferable framework without making the
procedure sufficiently operational.

Review01 item-by-item status
----------------------------

1. Positioning within the existing literature -- MOSTLY ADDRESSED, BUT STILL NEEDS TIGHTENING.
Evidence: Table~\ref{tab:relwork} (lines 128--138) now compares prior benchmark conclusions with
what changes in this study, and the Discussion contains a new “Positioning against prior work”
passage (lines 663--671). The manuscript distinguishes scalar benchmarks, carbon-accounting
methods, and the proposed decision instrument rather than only repeating its results.

Remaining issue: the new positioning is a long bold paragraph rather than a navigable Discussion
subsection, and several novelty statements are broader than the literature search shown in the
paper. “None characterise”/“none report” and “the prior literature does not reach” are supported
only by three representative benchmark rows and no search boundary. “We are unaware of” in the
table caption is appropriately cautious, but the surrounding prose should use the same scope.
“We reproduce” the scalar-literature engine ordering also needs to be framed as a qualitative
comparison, since the prior studies and this workload are not identical experiments.

Recommended action: make the positioning passage a real subsection, qualify universal literature
negatives as “among the studies reviewed here,” and state the comparison dimensions and search
scope. Keep the three-part contribution claim, but tie each part to the exact evidence and avoid
calling a conditional comparison an accepted universal ordering.

2. Dialogue with recent Environment Systems and Decisions publications -- PARTIALLY TO MOSTLY
ADDRESSED.
Evidence: lines 122--124 now engage six papers from the target journal, and the six entries are
present in references.bib. The citations are connected to methodological choices: local criteria,
sensitivity analysis, smart-home instrumentation, IoT operational integrity, and demand response.

Remaining issue: the dialogue is mostly analogical rather than a close comparison of decision
objects, indicators, system boundaries, or validation practices. Three of the six papers are from
2019--2022, so calling all six “recent” in the response letter is imprecise in the context of the
current revision. The statement that grid intensity is “the single most uncertain parameter in any
carbon accounting” (line 122) is an unsupported universal claim; this paper does not quantify the
uncertainty of RAPL measurement, workload mix, PUE, or embodied-carbon factors.

Recommended action: describe them as selected ESD precedents, add one sentence for each explaining
the methodological relationship and boundary, and replace “single most uncertain parameter in any
carbon accounting” with the narrower claim actually shown by the grid sensitivity experiment.

3. Practical/scientific value and applicability beyond the evaluated environment -- PARTIALLY
ADDRESSED, with important overclaiming remaining.
Evidence: the manuscript adds a fixed-cost/payload-cost mechanism and a local bracketing procedure
(lines 561--594), a second-host experiment (lines 387--428), a real-camera corpus (lines 431--489),
and a worked annual application (lines 598--646). It also explicitly says exact crossover payloads
do not generalise (line 671), which is a good correction to the original scope.

Remaining issue A: the proposed “procedure” is prose, not an operational decision protocol. It does
not specify how many payload points to test, how to select the winner under uncertainty, how to set
operation weights, or how to treat infeasible cells. A reader can understand the idea but cannot
apply the framework reproducibly without reconstructing those choices.

Remaining issue B: the evidence does not isolate all three dimensions named by the editor. The
second-host comparison changes processor, memory, storage, OS point release, container runtime,
Python patch level, and other environment details (lines 403--418 and 488--506). The real-frame
validation is performed on the second host, not the controlled host, so it jointly changes payload
and environment; it does not establish hardware-independent real-frame ordering. The sentence
“The architectural ordering does not” (line 488) should be limited to the tested host/corpus pair.

Remaining issue C: the annual deployment result is a scenario projection, not a measured saving in
the operational system. The paper acknowledges this at lines 646--647 because the installation
retains video rather than individual frames. The practical value is therefore a worked translation
of measured rates to a plausible deployment, not evidence that the current installation would save
the stated amount.

Recommended action: add a short numbered protocol or algorithm for local calibration; state that
the second-host and real-case experiments vary multiple factors jointly; and label the annual case
as a “scenario projection for the deployment workload.” Reserve “hardware independent,” “software
independent,” and “confirmed beyond our environment” for claims actually isolated by the design.

4. Limitations in the abstract -- ADDRESSED, BUT NOT COMPLETE.
Evidence: the abstract explicitly names the single-host/single-client baseline, limited corpus and
1080p real-frame ceiling, one grid intensity, and first-order embodied/fleet estimates (line 87).
This directly answers the editor's explicit request.

Remaining issue: the abstract does not say that the measurements cover the storage/data tier only,
not the deployment's face-recognition or inference pipeline, although that boundary is important in
the real-case section (lines 431--432 and 673). It also omits the single integrated measurement per
cell and the uncertainty/host variability that the paper later acknowledges. These omissions do not
invalidate the response, but they leave the practical scope easier to overread than the body allows.

Recommended action: add a compact limitation phrase stating that the real-case figures exclude
capture/inference and represent a scenario projection, while preserving the abstract's current
brevity.

5. AI-use declaration -- MOSTLY ADDRESSED.
Evidence: the Statements and Declarations section (line 691) says generative AI was used only for
language editing, excludes data analysis, figures, tables, and conclusions, and records author
review and responsibility. This is materially transparent and directly responds to review01.

Remaining issue: the declaration says “Generative AI tools” but does not identify the tool or tools.
The revision plan and response-letter draft specifically contemplated naming the tool. If the
journal requires tool identification, the current wording is incomplete; if it does not, the
purpose-and-boundary disclosure is otherwise adequate.

Recommended action: name the actual tool(s) used, or deliberately state that no specific tool name
is required by the journal's policy after checking the submission instructions.

Paper-level inconsistencies remaining after the review01 revisions
------------------------------------------------------------------

P1. HIGH -- The paper's strongest generality language exceeds its evidence.
STATUS: OPEN.
The abstract says the ordering is confirmed in a second computing environment, the real-case host
paragraph says the ordering does not change, and the conclusion says the real-case result is measured
on a second computer (lines 87, 488, and 681). What is demonstrated is: the controlled collage
ordering is compared across two non-identical environments, and the real-frame ordering is observed
on the smaller environment across the tested cameras. The paper does not run the same real corpus on
both hosts, nor hold the complete software environment constant. Reword “hardware independence” and
“confirmed” as “consistent across the tested host/corpus combinations.”

P2. HIGH -- The framework's decision objective is not consistently foregrounded in headline prose.
STATUS: OPEN.
The paper now correctly distinguishes equal one-insertion/one-bulk/one-point-read totals from
ingestion-only continuous retention (abstract, Table~\ref{tab:decision}, and lines 640--646), but
“greenest,” “choice,” and “crossover” still appear in places where the operation weights are not
repeated. A reader can incorrectly treat MongoDB-through-1440p, the real-case 1080p result, and the
annual PostgreSQL recommendation as contradictory. Every headline recommendation and figure
caption should state whether it means equal-operation total, ingestion-only, read-heavy, or storage
amplification objective.

P3. MEDIUM-HIGH -- The 6K infeasibility qualification is not propagated consistently.

Section~\ref{subsec:bson} carefully limits the empirical failure to the tested native time-series
configuration and default bucketing, but the abstract, conclusion, and Discussion still use broader
phrasing such as “cannot store 6K frames at all,” “no hardware,” and “hard infeasibility result.”
The immutable BSON document ceiling is general; the observed multi-frame bucket failure is
configuration-dependent. Use the qualified wording everywhere, especially in the abstract and final
contribution claim.

P4. MEDIUM -- The paper distinguishes a framework from a benchmark, but does not provide a compact
formal decision rule.
STATUS: OPEN.
The prose model at lines 561--594 explains fixed versus payload-dependent cost and deliberately
rejects a global linear fit. That is scientifically reasonable, but the paper should still give a
minimal rule: measure payload, choose an operation profile, measure each architecture at bracketing
payloads, compare means with uncertainty, and exclude infeasible cells. Without this, “transferable
crossover rule” sounds stronger than the actual deliverable, which is a transferable calibration
procedure.

P5. MEDIUM -- The real-case evidence is not fully exposed in the main paper.

The real-case table reports only the networked-camera comparison and only MongoDB's carbon/amplification
columns (Table~\ref{tab:realframes}); the full three-architecture values and the second-camera
summary are left to figures or prose. Since the paper uses these values to justify a changed

make the full relevant values visible in the manuscript.

Page-length constraint: this does not require a new workload-validation experiment or a full new
section in the main paper. If the additional values are needed, place them in a supplement or the

P6. MEDIUM -- The privacy/ethics wording is improved but the availability claim remains fragile.

The ethics paragraph correctly says that no detectable face is not proof of no personal data (lines

STATUS: OPEN.

Final paper-focused verdict
---------------------------
The editor's five requests are visibly present in main-marked.tex, so review01 has not been ignored.
Items 1, 2, and 3 should be described as substantially addressed but still requiring calibration of
claims; item 4 is addressed but can better state the data-tier boundary; item 5 is addressed in
substance but should name the AI tool if required. The paper is not yet ready to call those requests
fully closed until P1--P7 are resolved, especially the distinction between a local calibration
procedure and a universally transferable framework, and between a real deployment scenario and a
measured deployment saving.

Minimum revision path under the current page constraint
--------------------------------------------------------
The compiled marked PDF is already 44 A4 pages. Generalizability should therefore be addressed by
compact revision rather than by adding another workload study. The minimum paper change is:

1. Add a short, approximately 100--150-word calibration procedure: measure payload, define the
   operation profile, test the architectures at bracketing payloads, repeat/report uncertainty, and
   exclude infeasible cells.
2. Replace “hardware independent,” “software independent,” and “transferable crossover rule” with
   narrower evidence-based wording.
3. State that broader scenes, concurrency, batch sizes, and held-out deployments are future work;
   do not add those experiments to this revision.
4. Remove repeated explanatory prose in the Discussion to offset the added calibration paragraph.

No additional pages are required to give the editor a defensible generalizability response.
